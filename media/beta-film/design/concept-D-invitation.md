# Concept D: Done when

## 1. Title and logline

**Done when**

The people at Dorylinae write their closed-beta invitation the way your coding agents will write to each other: as a
three-line brief, *What / Why / Done when*. The film fills in *What* and *Why*, shows one real request travel from one
teammate's agent to another, and leaves *Done when* blank until the last chapter, when it is typed by hand:
"Your team applies."

Runtime **62.4 s** (26 bars at 100 BPM). Eleven shots in three chapters, and the chapters are the three fields of the
product's own brief template (`agentnet request --help`, `capture/02-request-help.txt`):

| Chapter | Field | Time | Job |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | `What:` | 0.0 to 4.8 s | The invitation: a closed beta, for small teams. |
| II | `Why:` | 4.8 to 40.8 s | What Dorylinae is, then one real request shown step by step (what testers would test). |
| III | `Done when:` | 40.8 to 62.4 s | The terms, the brief completed ("Your team applies."), the boot, the address. |

## 2. Strategy

### 2.1 Why an invitation, and why written as a brief

A stranger scrolling X or LinkedIn needs four answers, and their order decides who keeps watching:
*is this for me, what is it, does it really work, where do I go.* A product film answers them in the order
2, 3, 1, 4. An invitation answers 1 first. "We are opening a closed beta for small teams" lets the right viewer
recognise themselves in the first second and lets everyone else leave without cost. For a recruitment film that is
a feature, not a loss. evidence.md (§5.3, phrasing implications) asks the film to "say who it is for in one line ...
so the wrong people self-select out".

The danger of a call-led film is that it becomes an advert with no product in it. Developers punish that
(evidence.md §4.1: Mighty & True's "a Netflix trailer for an API"; §5.3: 46 % of developers distrust AI tools,
Stack Overflow 2025). The brief device is the fix. The invitation is set in the product's own grammar from frame 0:
the field names `What:`, `Why:` and `Done when:` and the two placeholders
`<one or two sentences of context>` and `<how the other side knows it is finished>` are copied character for character
from `agentnet request --help` (`capture/02`). At 14.7 s the viewer sees the real brief that one agent
wrote for another (`capture/run/work/review-brief.md`, the same text as the `brief` field in `capture/11`), with the
same three field names in the same colour. Without being told, the viewer learns the product's central object, the
brief, by reading an invitation written in it. At the end the open field is filled in. The call is the last line of
the brief.

This is also honest. The brief template is real, the request is real, and the only words the people at Dorylinae
"type" are the ones that are theirs to type.

### 2.4 Balance: explaining, showing, calling, and why

- **Calling takes its extra time from explaining, never from showing.** The demonstration keeps the same share
  evidence.md recommends (42 % against 43 %). A stranger cannot want to test something they have not seen work.
  Product-usage video beats appearance (Frontiers 2022), process beats outcome (Ringler 2024), and developers judge
  tools by trying them (Stack Overflow: free trial 75 %). The demo is the one block this concept does not shrink.
- **Explaining is shorter because it is spread out.** Only three sentences explain before the product appears. After
  that, every demo shot carries one plain sentence in the same invitation voice ("Your agent writes the brief.",
  "It goes to your teammate's agent." ...). The explanation runs through the whole film as captions on real material.
- **Calling is longer because of the end card and the terms.** 9.6 s of the 21.6 s is the boot and the address, which
  craft.md 1.12 and the 4-second end-card rule require anyway. The other 12 s carry the terms the right teams need
  before applying ("The first wave opens with 10 teams.", "Free during the beta.", "macOS, Linux and Windows.") and the
  signature moment.
- **The call never interrupts the demo.** It sits in the tag (36 px, `text-3`, never moving), so the demo frames still
  have one idea each.

### 2.5 How it uses craft.md

- **One sentence per shot, one idea per shot** (1.2, 1.7). Each shot has one entrance, one action and one hold.
- **No intro card** (1.12, Lago). The film opens on its claim. The mark appears only in the boot at the end (4.3 A).
- **The 21° cut is used exactly twice** (1.10). The first takes us from the invitation sheet into the product (9.6 s).
  The second takes us from the product back to the invitation (40.8 s). Every other change is a hard cut on a beat or a
  camera move.
- **Typing grammar** (2.2). Agent commands land whole in one frame. Output streams line by line. The only line typed
  letter by letter at the 85 ms boot cadence is the human ask, "Your team applies." It is the only typed line in the
  film, so it is the one that is felt.
- **Signature moves** (4.3). A, lock-in: every plate and the specimen label arrive with the boot's offsets and ease,
  and the real boot closes the film. B, the pheromone trail: it appears only for the two real events that travel, the
  request and the result. C, the specimen label: used once, for the sender, "From the people at Dorylinae". It is the
  determination label on the invitation.
- **Reading time** (5.4). Every line meets the floor rule. Every key claim meets the key-claim rule in the shot or
  across its repeats (copy deck, §4).
- **Honesty rules** (7). Terminal text is copied from `capture/` with the allowed edits only. No harness names, no
  versions, no paths, no relay URL, no drawn notifications.
- **Average shot length** is 5.3 s, excluding the end card. That is slower than the craft's 3.6 to 4.8 s, because an
  invitation is read, not watched. Camera moves inside the long shots (S4, S8, S10) keep them from standing still.

### 2.6 Cutdowns (for later)

The brief structure cuts down cleanly. **15 s:** S1 (What, 3.0 s), S5 (the request leaves, 4.8 s), S10 from the typed
line (3.6 s), and the address with no boot (3.6 s). **30 s:** S1, S3, S4, S5, S7, S10, end card.

## 5. The signature moment

**The brief completes itself (S10, 47.4 to 52.8 s).**

At 5 s the viewer sees an empty field at the bottom of the invitation: `Done when:` followed by the product's own
placeholder, `<how the other side knows it is finished>`. At 18 s, inside the real brief one agent sends another, the
same words `Done when:` get the only pheromone mark in the frame. At 46.8 s the film returns to that empty field. The
placeholder clears, and for the first and only time in the film a line is typed by hand, one letter every 85 ms:
**"Your team applies."** The last note of a three-note phrase lands with it. Then the camera pulls back, and the viewer
sees that the whole invitation was one brief, written in exactly the form the product uses: *What: We are opening a
closed beta for small teams. Why: Each of you keeps your own coding agent. Dorylinae lets the agents hand each other
work. Done when: Your team applies.*

Why people would remember and share it:
- **It is a real idea, not an effect.** The people at Dorylinae invite teams the way the product asks agents to ask
  each other. Developers notice when a company writes its call in its own product's grammar, and they repost things
  they noticed.
- **The poster frame is the film.** One still frame (S10 poster) carries the claim, the audience, the product, the call
  and the address. It is the thumbnail, the image under a repost, and what is left in the head of someone who watched
  half.
- **It is honest.** The template and placeholder are copied from `agentnet request --help`. Nothing is invented and
  nothing winks. The line is typed plainly and left to stand.

## 7. Risks, and what would make it fail

1. **The hook states instead of showing.** Evidence.md asks for "one concrete, recognisable situation shown, not told"
   in the first seconds. This concept opens on words. The first terminal arrives at 9.6 s, and "coding agent" lands at
   5.64 s, just after the 3 to 5 s subject target. The bet is that, for the target viewer, being named ("small teams",
   "each of you keeps your own coding agent") is the recognisable situation, and that "closed beta" is a stronger
   stop than any product shot. *Test:* if 3-second retention is poor, swap in a 4:5 variant that opens on S5's
   request line for 1.2 s before S1. The structure survives this.

2. **The brief device is missed, or reads as a gimmick.** If viewers do not connect `What:` / `Why:` / `Done when:`
   on the sheet with the real brief in S4, the film is still a clear invitation followed by a clear demo, so nothing
   breaks. The device fails only if it is oversold. Never add copy explaining it ("Our invitation is a brief too"),
   never animate the field names, and keep "Your team applies." plain.

3. **Too much calling for a cold audience.** At 35 % of the runtime plus a tag that is on screen for 76 %, the film
   could read as an advert. Keep the tag still, small and `text-3`. If testing shows fatigue, shorten S9 to 4.8 s and
   move "macOS, Linux and Windows." to the end card's small print.

4. **Pace.** The average shot is 5.3 s, slower than the craft's guide. If the animatic drags, the slack is in the S1
   push, the S3 hold and the S9 push. Never cut reading time below the floors in §4.

5. **Special Elite density in the poster frame.** Seven Special Elite lines under three field names may be judged a
   paragraph under the brand rule "nothing longer than a line or two". Each sentence is at most two lines and the field
   names break them apart, but a brand review should look at the S10 poster frame specifically. Fallback: set the Why
   values in the poster at `text-3`.

6. **Honesty edges for the fact-checker.**
   - **S4.** The brief is shown as the file `review-brief.md` (from `capture/run/work/`), not as terminal output. The
     same text is in the captured `request show --json`.
   - **S6.** "When" is in the caption, but the timestamp is cropped off the relay line.
   - **S8.** The film skips round 1 and the change request. The omitted output block would show "Re-reviewed", so the
     shown lines stay true.
   - **S3.** The status table shows HUMAN `unknown` as texture. It is real and implies nothing.
   - **S5.** The idempotency key reads `demo-review-1`. It is kept because the command must be the one that ran.
   - **The trail's travel time** is a diagram, not a speed claim: both machines were online and delivery took
     milliseconds. No caption claims speed.

7. **4:5 crops.** The brief values, inbox titles and the relay line crop at the right frame edge. In S6 the `from=`
   key is partly cut, so "who" leans on the `to=` key and the caption. Check every crop on a phone at arm's length.

8. **The terms land after the midpoint.** Viewers who leave at 31 s miss "10 teams" and "free". They still have the
   call and the address. If the owner prefers, "Free during the beta." can join the tag ("Free during the beta. Apply
   at dorylinae.net"). That costs the tag's identical wording, so the end card would have to match.

9. **External dependency.** The film assumes a live application page at dorylinae.net on publish day
   (beta-facts §5). Without it the whole call fails.

10. **Execution traps.**
    - A soft or blurred 21° edge reads as a stock wipe.
    - Light leaks or glow on the label-stock card would cheapen it.
    - A caret, or typing sounds, on "Your team applies." would turn the one human moment into a gimmick.
    - A sentimental motif, or one that is too loud, would do the same.
    - If anyone picks a mid-push frame as the thumbnail instead of the S10 poster frame, the summary is lost.
